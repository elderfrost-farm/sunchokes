Why grow it

The most food for the least work

It's the best calorie-for-effort crop I grow. A hundred feet of row gives you about 250 pounds of tubers, and federal trials put commercial plantings at 12,000–22,000 pounds per acre. You plant it once.

It stores itself

The tops die at first frost. The tubers don't — they stay alive in the ground all winter and get sweeter as starch turns to sugar. No cellar, no canning, no freezer. Dig what you need before the ground freezes, then again as soon as it thaws in spring.

Native, and covered in bees

Helianthus tuberosus comes from the Great Lakes and Mississippi valleys. Late in the season it throws yellow daisy flowers, right when the rest of the garden has quit, and the pollinators come. The tall stalks double as a windbreak and hold a slope against erosion.

Good to eat, easy on blood sugar

Raw it's crisp like jicama. Roasted it's nutty and sweet, with a finish that tastes like an artichoke heart. Per 100 g: 73 calories, 429 mg potassium, 3 mg iron, and a glycemic index of 32 — potatoes are in the eighties. Its fiber is inulin, a prebiotic at 10–22% of fresh weight, the most of any vegetable.

And it asks for almost nothing

No fertilizer, no sprays, no watering. It tolerates drought and frost, thrives in poor soil, and has no real pests or diseases. It'll grow on ground you wouldn't bother to plough.

Before you plant

Two things to know

Neither is a dealbreaker. Both are easy to handle — as long as you know about them before your first harvest, not after.

01 The gas

Inulin is a fiber your small intestine can't digest. It reaches your colon, bacteria ferment it, and you get gas. That's exactly what makes it a good prebiotic — and why a big bowl of raw sunchokes can ruin an evening.

It's a preparation problem, not a flaw. Cook them with something acidic, cook them long, ferment them, or just start with small portions.

Cook them properly and start slowly, and it's a non-issue. Eat half a pound raw on the first night and it isn't.

02 The spread

Once established they spread 2–4 feet a year, and any fragment of tuber left in the soil comes up. It's the same vigor that makes them yield so heavily, so you don't fight it — you plan for it.

My method: at harvest I take every large tuber. That leaves more than enough to regrow the whole patch, for free, every year.

Give them their own bed with a mown border and harvest hard, and you'll never have a problem. Plant them mid-garden and you'll fight them for a decade.

Keeping them where you put them

  • Give them their own bed. Not a corner of the vegetable garden — a separate bed with a mown strip at least two feet wide around it. That strip is the containment.
  • Harvest hard every year. Take every large tuber. What you miss regrows the patch for free and rarely crosses the border.
  • No spare ground? Sink a container. A twenty-inch pot in the ground still gives a real yield and stays put.
  • Use the ground you can't farm. Wet corner, steep bank, rough fill — that's sunchoke ground.
  • If they win anyway, smother them: cardboard, six inches of mulch, one full season.

Plant · Grow · Harvest

From a bag of tubers to a standing patch, in one season

1 — Planting

  • WhenEarly spring, as soon as the ground can be worked. Fall works too.
  • Depth4 inches, eyes facing up
  • In-row12–18 inches apart
  • Between rows24–36 inches; wider for tall varieties
  • SoilpH 6.0–7.5, well drained, any texture
  • SunFull sun — six hours minimum
  • FertilizerNone. Too much nitrogen gives you ten feet of stem and no tubers.

The one way to kill them is wet feet — standing water rots tubers fast. If your bed holds water in spring, raise it.

2 — Growing

They come up fast and keep going — five to ten feet by August. A thick stand makes a real windbreak and a real privacy screen, which is a useful thing to get free with a vegetable.

Water matters in one window: late August through flowering, while the tubers are bulking. Miss it and you get smaller tubers. Our 2025 crop came through a Northeast drought, which is why those tubers run small — and why you get more of them per pound.

Deer browse the tops. Voles eat tubers left in the ground. Those are the only two animals worth worrying about.

3 — Harvesting

Wait for the tops to die back and dry. Until then the plant is still sending nutrients down into the tubers — harvest early and you get a smaller crop that can taste bitter.

After that, the tubers keep themselves — right where they are, getting sweeter with the cold. Dig what you want before the ground freezes in, then again as soon as it thaws. Want to dig mid-winter? Mulch the bed heavily in the fall.

Take all the big ones and leave the rest. That's the whole system — the patch replants itself, better than you would.

In the kitchen

Cooks like a potato. Tastes like something better.

Raw, the flesh is crisp and white — closer to jicama than potato. Cooked, it goes soft and sweet with a finish that tastes like an artichoke heart. That's the flavor the English were chasing in 1841 when they turned it into potage à la Palestine.

Raw & shaved

Mandoline thin, ice water, drain hard. Lemon, good oil, flaky salt. The salad that converts people.

Roasted with lemon

Halve, toss with oil, salt and a good squeeze of lemon. 425°F until the edges caramelise. Don't skip the lemon.

Soup

Simmer with onion and stock, a splash of vinegar, blend, finish with cream. Deeper than potato soup, and it freezes well.

Pickled & fermented

Diced into brine with garlic, turmeric, ginger and cumin. A week on the counter, then the fridge. This is the one that wins over people who thought they couldn't eat sunchokes.

Mashed

Half sunchoke, half potato. The potato gives body, the sunchoke gives flavor. Butter, and plenty of it.

The whole plant

All of it's edible — young shoots, leaves (hairy until they've cooked five minutes), flower buds, and the unripe seeds.

First time? Cook with lemon or vinegar, keep it to a few tubers, and give your gut a week to adjust. Then scale up.
